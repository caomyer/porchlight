Porchlight proposal · Nov 15, 2026 · for review

Porchlight in your pocket

What a phone companion is for, what the rest of the market does with theirs, how the wall changes once phones join, and what it takes to build. The mocks below are live: tap the buttons on the phone and the wall.

The wall shows us. The phone shows me, and lets me pitch in from anywhere. The wall stays the family's shared page, and the place kids use. Each grown-up's phone already knows who they are, so it can drop the "who did it" step, carry the reminders that are theirs, and log the walk or the meal where it happens. Everything still works on the wall alone; phones add to it and never replace it.
1 · What the market does

Every family display treats the phone as the remote control

The display companies (Skylight, Hearth, Cozyla) use the phone to set things up and the screen to glance and tap. Phone-first apps (Cozi, TimeTree, FamilyWall) have no shared screen at all. The closest match to Porchlight's care logging is a small pet app, Fed?, built on iCloud with no account.

Kids don't need phones

Every display product keeps kids as tap-on-the-wall profiles. Skylight even sells a separate kid tablet instead of a kid app. The wall stays the kids' device.

Logging wins on the lock screen

The best-reviewed care app logs from widgets and the Lock Screen without opening the app. A one-tap "I walked Yo-Yo" belongs there.

Approval queues backfire

Chore apps that ping a parent to approve each task create the notification overload people complain about most. We trust the tap, and the wall keeps undo.

Paywalls and outages hurt

The most common complaints are paying to use basic features, no offline mode, and a hub that silently goes stale. Local-first iCloud sync avoids all three.

The wall is the family's page. The phone is yours.

5 · What it takes

Mostly a data-layer project; the phone screens are the easy part

Today every row lives in one SQLite file on the iPad with auto-increment ids, and the app is signed with a free Apple ID that expires every 7 days. Sync needs both to change. The phone itself reuses the existing stores, components and theme. The build order, with a gate for each step, is the delivery plan in the UX atlas; the phases below are the outline.

0 · FoundationWall only, no visible change
Largest
  • Apple Developer Program: done (Nov 15). CloudKit, push and TestFlight are unlocked, and the wall's install now lasts a year instead of 7 days.
  • Move the data to sync-ready ids. All 21 tables switch from auto-increment row ids to UUIDs in one migration, under a single Household root.
  • Adopt SQLiteData (Point-Free). It is built on GRDB, which the app already uses, and syncs to CloudKit with sharing. Its rules fit our schema after small changes: every table hangs off one parent chain, so references like "which person" (in careLogPerson, journalEntryEater, choreCompletion) become plain id columns rather than foreign keys.
  • Photos become iCloud assets. Meal photos sync as their own rows. The photo frame's library can stay local at first.
  • Free bonus: the household is backed up to iCloud. A dead or replaced iPad no longer loses years of walks and meals.
1 · Phone v1What the mock shows
Medium
  • Same app, two shells. One universal target: iPad opens the wall, iPhone opens the pocket shell with four tabs. Features keep one store each and gain phone-sized views.
  • Join by QR. The wall shows an iCloud share link as a QR code. The phone accepts it, picks who you are, and remembers that on the device only.
  • Today, Food, Lists, Glow as mocked. The calendar on the phone shows today's household events from the phone's own calendars. Events are still created in Apple or Google Calendar.
  • Reminders computed on the phone. Each phone schedules local notifications for its owner's routine windows and cancels them when anyone logs. No server needed, and they arrive offline.
  • One interactive widget (WidgetKit + App Intents, iOS 17): "I walked Yo-Yo" without opening the app.
2 · LaterAfter living with v1
Small each
  • Remind someone from the wall (needs a push between devices; see the risk below).
  • Send photos to the frame from the Photos share sheet.
  • Lock Screen and Control Center logging; Apple Watch for walks.
  • A walk timer on the Lock Screen.
Everyone needs an iPhone

CloudKit sharing is Apple-only. An Android phone in the household would need our own server or a web page, which is a different project.

"Remind Mia" is best-effort

Without a server, a nudge travels as a CloudKit change notification. iOS may delay these silent pushes by minutes. Fine for "can you walk Yo-Yo tonight", not for urgent alerts.

The owner's iCloud holds the data

Shared data counts against the owner's iCloud storage. Meal photos are the bulk; we should keep them compressed (about 300 KB each).

Calendars differ per device

Calendar ids are local to each device, so the "this calendar is James's" mapping has to match on calendar name and account instead.

Sync is hard to test

End-to-end tests need two simulators signed into two iCloud test accounts. We should add a fake sync engine for unit tests and keep a short manual checklist on real devices.

6 · iCloud sync, designed for any household

Nothing in the sync knows about our family, our wall, or our dog

Sync is built for every household that installs Porchlight: phone-only families, two homes sharing kids, grandparents who join from afar. Our wall iPad is just one way to use it.

A household is what gets shared

One household record owns everything else. Sharing it with iCloud invites someone in with read and write access. A person can belong to several households (co-parents in two homes, grandparents), and the app switches between them.

Any device can start one

Whoever creates the household owns it, on any device. For us that is the wall iPad; for a phone-only family it is a parent's iPhone. Invites go out as a link by QR code, Messages or AirDrop.

Device role is a setting

"Shared screen" means anyone can tap, so it asks who did it. "Personal" means it credits its owner. iPads ask during setup; iPhones default to personal. Nothing branches on iPad versus iPhone.

Your data, your iCloud

Data lives only in the families' own iCloud. We run no server and create no accounts, and we can't read anyone's data. The App Store privacy label can say "Data Not Collected".

Rules every table follows, checked by tests

1UUID ids. Two phones can create rows offline without colliding.
2One parent per row. Each table hangs off the household by a single chain (household, companion, routine, log), which is what iCloud sharing can follow. References across the tree, like "who did it" or "which restaurant", are plain ids that the app resolves and that degrade gracefully if missing.
3No unique constraints; derived ids instead. "The food page for Tuesday" or "the chore done on Tuesday" get an id computed from their parent and day. Two devices writing the same day land on the same row and merge, instead of creating duplicates.
4Actions are new rows. Walks, chore checks and cash-ins append to a log rather than flipping a field, so two people acting at once never overwrite each other. Edits to the same field merge as "last edit wins", field by field.
5People and places are archived, never deleted. A year of history keeps its names even after someone moves out.
6Big data in its own tables. Photos, thumbnails and Pencil pages sync as iCloud files, so lists and timelines stay fast. A phone fetches full-size photos only when you open one.
7Some things stay on the device. Calendar ids (which differ per device), kiosk and night settings, "who am I", and the photo frame's own library never sync. Calendars are shared as "this account's Family calendar belongs to Mia" and matched on each device.
8The schema only grows. We add tables and optional columns, and never rename or remove, so an iPad that hasn't updated keeps working with a phone that has. A test compares the schema with the last shipped one and fails on anything else.
9Nothing assumes who the owner is. The household is known by its own id, never by the iCloud account or share that holds it. People link to members, not to iCloud users, and "who am I" points at a person. The owner is only a fact we read from the share, so moving a household to another member later means copying it and re-inviting, with no schema change. A test copies a household into a fresh iCloud stand-in and checks that every row and link survives.

Every failure has a designed state

What happensWhat people see
Offline, or iCloud is downEverything works; changes queue and send later. A quiet "Last synced 2 h ago" appears only after a long gap, never a spinner.
Signed out of iCloudThe household keeps working on this device only, with a one-line notice in Settings explaining how to turn sync back on.
The owner's iCloud is fullChanges keep saving on each device. The owner sees the storage card below with "iCloud is full" and the ways to free space; everyone else sees "Paused: James's iCloud is full", and sync resumes on its own once there is room.
Removed from a household, or the owner stops sharingThe household disappears from that device after one notice. A future option can save a copy first.
Two edits at onceEach field keeps its latest edit; logs and checks never collide, by rule 4.
Different app versionsUnknown new data is kept and shown once the app updates, by rule 8.

Storage, in plain view

Everything the household shares counts against the owner's iCloud, and only theirs. Members use almost none of their own, so a free 5 GB account is enough to join. Apple doesn't let apps read how much iCloud space is left, so the meter shows what the household itself uses and how fast it grows, and the app learns the account is full when iCloud says so.

·Guidance at setup. Creating a household says plainly that it is stored in your iCloud and grows mainly with photos, so the person with the most iCloud room should create it.
·Photos sync at a sensible size. A 2048-pixel copy (a few hundred KB) syncs; the full original stays on the device that took it. That keeps a busy year of food photos well under 1 GB.
·A heads-up before it bites. When growth would fill a typical plan within a few months, or iCloud first reports it's full, the owner gets one notice with the card above. No repeat nagging.
·Counted locally. Each data table records its file size, so the meter is a quick sum on any device with no extra iCloud calls.
